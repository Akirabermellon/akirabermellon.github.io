Inside true crime journalism

By Akira Salas ArizaOctober 20, 2023
The Restless Eye
Narrative reporting on current affairs.

“There is no human story that is small.” The journalist begins as a witness to events and becomes a translator of the world. Armed with a name, a surname, and at most a face, the reporter delves abruptly into the harsh reality of their sources so that it reaches ordinary mortals, their readers. Being a crime reporter demands a brave intruder; but as Cruz Morcillo puts it: “Evil exists, and someone has to tell it.”

The green velvet seats fill as attendees settle in. It is October 20 at the VIII International Journalism Congress in MÁlaga. Three seasoned journalists take the stage: Ángeles EscrivÁ, Cruz Morcillo, and ÍÑigo DomÍnguez, moderated by JosÉ MarÍa Loma. Together, they dissect the fine line between investigative rigor, empathy, and sensationalism in modern crime reporting.

“Crime journalism is not about feeding morbid curiosity; it is about examining the deepest fractures of society,” EscrivÁ remarks. Reporting on violent crimes, terrorism, or systemic corruption forces journalists to navigate intense ethical dilemmas—balancing the public's right to know against the privacy and dignity of victims.

Cruz Morcillo reflects on the relationship with sources: from police investigators to judicial contacts and grieving families. “You cannot deceive your sources, nor can you turn your report into a sensationalist spectacle. Rigor is your only shield.”

ÍÑigo DomÍnguez emphasizes the critical role of context: “A crime story without social and institutional context is merely sensationalism. True crime reporting must question why the system failed and what structural vulnerabilities allowed the tragedy to occur.”

The session concludes with a shared realization: far from sensationalist entertainment, investigative crime journalism remains one of the most demanding and essential branches of the craft, demanding rigorous fact-checking, ethical restraint, and uncompromising storytelling.
