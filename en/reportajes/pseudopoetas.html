Pseudopoets and automatic writing

By Akira Salas ArizaFebruary 18, 2024
The Restless Eye
Narrative reporting on current affairs.

Marta Synés first opened her eyes in the city where there is nothing more sorrowful than being blind, as Francisco de Icaza famously wrote. Federico García Lorca once noted: «Granada is made for dreams and daydreams; on every side it borders the ineffable… Granada will always be more plastic than philosophical, more lyrical than dramatic». Yet she did not confine herself to a single art form. Poet, film director, and screenwriter: at twenty-five, Marta Synés already has three published books and over ten short films behind her.

Her latest poetry collection, Las flores de la carne (2023), approaches bodily identity, intimacy, and pain with disarming vulnerability. In her conversation with Akira Salas Ariza, she reflects on the modern landscape of creative writing, the emergence of algorithmic composition, and the essential value of raw human honesty in contemporary literature.

“Writing is an act of exposure,” Synés explains. “When you strip away superficial ornamentation and confront raw reality, words cease to be an intellectual exercise and become a shared visceral experience.”

She challenges current literary trends prioritizing viral aesthetics over substance: “There is an inflation of pseudopoetry designed for instant digital consumption. True literature requires patience, friction, and an unyielding commitment to emotional truth.”

Feminism remains a primary catalyst for her writing. Identifying as a radical feminist focused on dissecting the root structures of patriarchy, she explicitly champions trans-inclusive feminism, refusing to tolerate discrimination against trans people.

She revisits this in her poem «26 de abril», commemorating the landmark verdict in the *La Manada* case—a moment of collective awakening where women took an unequivocal stance. While recognizing that watershed moment, she expresses disillusionment with recent commodified demonstrations: «That day was not for dancing; it was for screaming until your throat bled». She laments how commercial interests have co-opted political urgency.

Looking ahead, Synés is developing a project exploring grief and community support networks. Having lost three direct family members to cancer—her uncles in childhood, her 90-year-old grandfather, and recently her aunt—alongside supporting another aunt through domestic abuse recovery, she emphasizes the necessity of unconditional emotional support. Her mission is to write to connect, fostering empathy and helping readers understand the transformative power of human solidarity.
