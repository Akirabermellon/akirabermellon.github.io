The situation of women in the West Bank during Covid-19: Palestine under Israeli occupation, patriarchy, and the pandemic

By Akira Salas ArizaField Investigation and Reporting
The Restless Eye
Narrative reporting on current affairs.

From above, the buildings resemble stacked, hollow boxes. The horizon is veiled in dark grey dust, below which olive groves and protected green pine forests stretch out. In the distance, rising without warning, lies the monumental city of Tel Aviv—ancient Jaffa.

From the sky, the dividing lines that frame Palestine are unmistakable. In a landscape conquered by modern settlements encroaching upon towns and villages, socioeconomic divides cutting through the territories are readily apparent. Restored houses inspired by European designs with pitched roofs disrupt an ancient reality.

The new inhabitants are strangers. The Israeli government grants them incentives and low prices to establish colonies on usurped territory. On the highest elevations where the world is watched, settlers live where olive trees no longer grow. Now there are only foreign trees pretending to have belonged there longer than the true owners of the land.

Walls trace property boundaries from one plot to another, creating imagined yet tangible borders that disfigure Palestinian lands and prevent the free movement of their inhabitants. This architecture stretches for more than 600 kilometers, and its route isolates cities and villages, tears apart farmlands, and breaches properties—frequently making it impossible for Palestinians to access essential services or causing delays of several hours.

The barrier prevents many landowners from accessing their fields, and others from reaching the resources needed to care for them. Deep water wells sit on the other side of the wall, and these, along with other hydraulic resources, remain under Israeli control as part of its asset appropriation policy (Pérez, 2023).

The wall takes the shape of concrete slabs, but also barbed wire and electrified fences. Its construction began in 2002 and, although declared illegal by the International Court of Justice and the United Nations, continues to expand daily across Palestinian territory. Its presence is not solely a physical barrier, but as Menéndez (2009) asserts, a constant and symbolic reminder of Israeli imposition.

Distances carry entirely different meanings depending on whether you belong to the Palestinian people or Israeli citizenship. In other words, traveling from Ben Gurion Airport in Tel Aviv to the city of Jerusalem—recognized as the capital of Israel by Trump in 2017 and claimed as the capital of Palestine as well—there is a direct train covering 42 kilometers without obstacles or barriers.

Meanwhile, the distance between Al-Walaja, a nearby town, to Jerusalem itself is just ten minutes by car along an Israeli highway. It is so close that from the upper part of the village one can distinguish the city's buildings; yet many Palestinians will never set foot on its streets, even when their homes are located in territory officially categorized as municipal Jerusalem.

The same happens in Al-E'izariya and Jabal Al-Baba, two towns located on the opposite side of Jerusalem: the wall has split neighborhoods in half, devouring land that belonged to Palestinian families. Family members who once lived across the street now require an hour's drive and a specialized permit just to touch each other again.

Bodour Hassam is a Palestinian activist, researcher, expert in the conflict, and advocate for women's rights. She affirms: “When people lose their land, they don't just lose their home—they lose their identity.” This generates generational voids that cannot be bridged. The trauma of occupation is a collective trauma. Every Palestinian knows abuse firsthand or closely and has lost a significant part of their family roots with it.

Land divisions, military outposts, checkpoints, and all the bureaucratic permits that form part of daily life have created vast chasms between families. Home can turn into an unreachable destination for anyone whose permit to return has been revoked.

Covid-19 imbued the spaces of Palestine with renewed severity. Confining the population to their homes, imposing abusive measures, and condemning them amid severe shortages of medical equipment and vaccines. There were 703,228 confirmed positive cases, and 5,708 Palestinians died due to Covid-19, according to the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics (PCBS) (2023).

The consequences for Palestinian laborers were equally severe, with the refugee population emerging as an especially vulnerable group due to physical and economic conditions. According to a United Nations report (2020), more than half of Palestinian refugees fell into poverty following the pandemic.

A fragmented country

Palestine is a fragmented country. Its territory is split into two regions: the West Bank and the Gaza Strip. Traveling from one to the other requires covering 93 kilometers across the territory of Israel. The Gaza Strip is enclosed on all its borders: encircled by a massive barrier, with its airspace forbidden and its maritime waters under constant surveillance. Its residents live amidst continuous armed conflict, governed by Hamas, which took power following the 2006 elections.

In the other occupied territory, the West Bank is steadily cornered. Its population is dispossessed of their land and besieged by settlements, where settlers use violence to compel people to abandon their houses. Mahmud Zwareh, an activist from Al-Masara who regularly protests settlement expansion, notes that settler numbers approach 850,000, while the PCBS estimated 657,747 settlers by 2021.

This policy represents an internal annexation that creates fractures dividing Palestinian society and isolating its communities. Cut into fragments and invaded from within, Palestinian spaces are shrinking pieces of a broken puzzle. Direct civil and military Palestinian control is confined to Area A, the smallest sector, encompassing just 18% of the territory.

Meanwhile, Area B features Palestinian civil administration but Israeli military control, covering 22% of the land. Finally, Area C—entirely controlled by Israel—spans 60% of the West Bank.

Palestine has been dispossessed of those who inhabited it: 5.9 million people have been displaced from their homes and turned into refugees according to the United Nations (UN). Around 190,000 live in West Bank and East Jerusalem refugee camps, according to the PLO Negotiations Affairs Department as of July 4, 2023.

Consequently, fragmentation isolates communities into distinct pockets, making mutual support difficult through the maze of obstacles engineered by Israel. Territorial divisions, checkpoints, concrete walls, and refugee displacement profoundly obstruct quality of life, employment, and housing, hindering communal solidarity and survival (RodrÍguez, 2019).

Moreover, fragmentation operates not only through physical partition, but also through diverse administrative regimes aimed at limiting collective resistance. The differing frameworks across Areas A, B, and C, combined with the distinct realities of cities, rural villages, and refugee camps, have fostered localized identities. Yet despite these divides, Palestine unites around a shared objective: ending the occupation.

The women

Manal Tamimi does not remember when she began resisting—perhaps because she has done so all her life. “I was born into a fighting family. My uncles were exiled to Jordan, many relatives were killed by the occupation, including my father... When you witness that, you don't deliberate; you follow in your family's footsteps,” the human rights defender explains.

Born in Nabi Saleh, Tamimi has demonstrated against the occupation throughout her life. In Palestine, one cannot live detached from politics: it is lived daily whenever crossing a military checkpoint and facing an armed soldier demanding identification.

Tamimi is also a mother, but her role extends far beyond traditional caregiving: women do whatever is required to confront the system of apartheid that oppresses them. “I don't remember exactly when I made the decision or if it just happened. But definitely, if I had had a choice, I would have chosen to resist.”

Amnesty International, the United Nations, and Human Rights Watch recognize that Israel is practicing apartheid against Palestine. As Amnesty International (2022) notes, apartheid occurs when serious human rights violations are committed within an institutionalized regime of systematic oppression and domination of one racial group over another. This is also codified in the International Convention on the Suppression and Punishment of the Crime of Apartheid and the Rome Statute.

The reality of Palestinian women is shaped by apartheid and entwined with patriarchal structures sustaining traditional gender roles. An analysis by Urrutia (2019) demonstrated that occupation dynamics exacerbate preexisting gender inequalities, creating specific vulnerabilities for women.

Furthermore, systemic state abuse reproduces explicit violence within the most intimate spheres of domestic life. As Andrade, Alvis, JimÉnez, Redondo, and RodrÍguez (2017) explain, in conflict scenarios, polarized dynamics often legitimize degrading behaviors against already vulnerable groups, increasing violence against women.

According to PCBS data (2020), 59.3% of married women experienced domestic violence in 2019 (18.5% physical violence, 9.4% sexual violence). More than half of married women were subjected to abuse by their partners that year.

Personal status legislation further compounds women's vulnerability. Matrimonial and divorce matters fall under religious courts, meaning a woman typically requires the husband's consent or must forfeit all financial and child custody rights to obtain a divorce. “Many men use this as a weapon to force women to remain in abusive marriages,” notes Bodour Hassam.

Child marriage also remains a concern: in 2022, 2.75% of marriages involved minors, and in 2021, 0.8% involved girls aged 14 or younger (PCBS, 2023).

The traditional role of women as caregivers takes on unique gravity under occupation, arbitrary arrests, and night raids (Urrutia, 2019). Hassam details that women bear full domestic responsibilities while also contributing to income-generating activities and managing household stress during house demolitions and utility cutoffs.

With a fertility rate of 3.8 children per woman in 2020 (PCBS, 2023), the maternal burden is psychological as well as physical: mothers must care for their children's education and health while simultaneously safeguarding them from being detained, injured, or killed.

Tamimi recounts: “When your children go to school, you don't know if they will return.” Children are frequently detained on their way to or from class, their backpacks left behind on the pavement. In 2015, thirteen-year-old Abed al-Rahman Shadi Obeidallah from Aida camp was shot dead by an Israeli soldier while still wearing his schoolbag.

“Under occupation, you don't just raise your children; you have to build strong children,” Tamimi asserts. She prepares her children through legal awareness, audio recordings of interrogations, and mock interrogation drills: “We hold them, blindfold them, and place them in a room with harsh interrogation audio. Then, they cry out.”

In 2022 alone, 150 Palestinian children were arrested and 53 killed by Israeli forces (PCBS, 2023). Tamimi emphasizes: “No matter how much you want to protect your children and keep them safe, under occupation anything can happen at any moment. At their age, I should be teaching them football or basketball, not how to endure tear gas or interrogation.”

Family forms the bedrock of Palestinian communal resilience and cultural continuity (Morande, 1999; MalÉ, 2021). Women sustain this primary collective resistance unit, enabling identity and survival under occupation.

Women are central organizers in weekly nonviolent protests. In Nabi Saleh, four women and five men coordinate demonstrations; twelve local women have been imprisoned. In 2022, 172 women were arrested and 18 killed by occupation forces across Palestine.

Bodour Hassam stresses horizontal solidarity: “International women can listen and exchange experiences without judging or trying to explain us. Understand that our circumstances differ and practice mutual solidarity.”

Youth activist Kate Khair adds: “To achieve women's freedom, political freedom and the liberation of society are essential.”

In Al-Walaja, Lubna Abualteen co-founded a women's carpentry collective using recycled timber, generating economic self-reliance across Bethlehem, Beit Sahur, Ramallah, and Gaza. “Many men doubted we could operate such heavy machinery,” Abualteen reflects, “but now they consult us on techniques, and we train women across communities.”

A healthcare system in pieces

The fragility of Palestinian medical relief was underscored when 21-year-old Abdel Fattah al-Sharif was executed at point-blank range by Israeli soldier Elor Azaria while lying gravely wounded on the ground, with ambulances driving past without administering aid.

Systemic interference degrades healthcare infrastructure (Barghouthi & Giacaman, 1993). Checkpoints frequently delay ambulances: WHO figures (2022) show that 94% of Red Crescent ambulance transfers to East Jerusalem required transferring patients into Israeli-licensed vehicles.

Retired physician Dr. Najic Nassar of Beit Sahur explains that public health, UNRWA, NGOs, and private clinics struggle under tight fiscal constraints, exacerbated by a -13.4% GDP contraction in 2020 (PCBS, 2021) and heavy donor dependence (12% of health expenditure; WHO, 2022).

During the pandemic, Israel was condemned by Amnesty International and the UN for denying vaccines to occupied Palestinians while leading global vaccination rates for its own citizens. When vaccines were eventually provided, many were near expiry. Dr. Nassar noted approximately 20,000 expired vaccines delivered, with vaccination posts strategically placed primarily to protect Israeli workers, culminating in a Palestinian vaccination rate of 36.6% compared to 87.2% in Spain.

The origin of the disease: The sacred city of Bethlehem

The first Covid-19 infections in Palestine originated with Greek tourists staying at the Angel Hotel in Beit Jala, Bethlehem. On March 6, 2020, surrounding hotel workers fell ill, triggering swift community lockdowns.

Workers returning home spread the infection to bustling souks and neighborhoods. Bethlehem, renowned for its luminous streets and narrow historic corridors, saw its commercial vitality halt abruptly as military closures tightened.

Lockdown measures resulted in 453,000 job losses within a month (Who Profits, 2020), disproportionately impacting women, 54% of whom experienced economic violence (PCBS, 2021). Online schooling strained households where device access was scarce despite 83% internet penetration.

In Bethlehem, a single public hospital with limited ventilators relied on improvised quarantine facilities in hotels and rehab centers. In Hebron, where settlements dissect the old city and poverty stood at 20.3% (PCBS, 2018), medical access was severely curtailed, heavily affecting pregnant women and prenatal care.

Gender-based violence surged during quarantine: 55% of women reported psychological violence at home during the initial months (UN Women, 2020; Zatara, 2020), as support services and courts were incapacitated.

Consequences in refugee camps: Overcrowding in Aida Camp

Aida refugee camp north of Bethlehem embodies chronic overcrowding under constant surveillance from the separation wall and military towers. With 42% of Palestinians holding refugee status and 23% in extreme poverty (UNRWA, 2023), social distancing proved impossible.

Um Ahed, a psychologist at the Aida Youth Center, organized women's mutual aid networks to bake daily bread and distribute cleaning supplies. Women assumed the exhausting burden of continuous domestic sanitization and family care, while 70.1% of service sector workers (health and education) in the West Bank were women (PCBS, 2021).

Psychological distress surged, with acute stress affecting 82% of women compared to 71% of men (UN Women, 2020). Despite formal pandemic pauses, Israeli military incursions persisted: soldiers in full HAZMAT suits conducted night raids, arresting camp residents at gunpoint beneath the symbolic Key of Return arch.

Sovereignty in Al-Masara and harassment in Nabi Saleh

While Nabi Saleh endured night tear gas raids, Al-Masara organized community defense and agricultural solidarity. “We transformed collective protest into daily acts of resistance: cultivating land and sharing harvests,” explains Mahmud Zwareh. “The pandemic taught us how to live on the essentials and assert sovereignty over our territory.”

Najwa Ibrahim, a 36-year-old pregnant mother in Al-Masara, contracted severe Covid-19 with failing oxygen levels. After Bethlehem's hospital was overwhelmed, she was transferred to Makassed Hospital in Jerusalem, spending four months in intensive care before giving birth to a healthy daughter.

Across the intricate tapestry of Palestine, the pandemic converged with military occupation and patriarchal constraints. Through every trial, Palestinian women remained the indispensable foundation of resilience, care, and community survival.
