The bloody repression of the Belarusian dictatorship

By Akira Salas ArizaJanuary 15, 2024
The Restless Eye
Narrative reporting on current affairs.

Four gunshots ring out. Several leave bullet holes piercing the traffic sign hanging above his head, and Juilu Kevrot collapses to the ground. He crawls several meters through the grass toward a tree. There he cowers, terrified. Only after half an hour does he dare take out his mobile phone and text: «I'm still alive».

While Kyiv reverberates with heavy explosions from Russian bombardments, NATO reports that up to 30,000 Russian troops are hosted by Belarus. Juliu Kevrot recalls that day as he watches fresh anti-war protests surging through the streets of Minsk, the capital. Over 100,000 citizens mobilized against the war; around 530 were officially arrested, though the democratic opposition estimates the real number is far higher.

Two years earlier, this same capital deafened its avenues with shouts demanding democracy. In the summer of 2020, the “Slipper Revolution” erupted across Belarus. Thirty thousand people took to the streets peacefully, and the state answered with savage brutality. Belarusian armed forces alongside mercenaries—both Russian and Serbian—savagely beat anyone resisting Aleksandr Lukashenko's grip on the presidency.

Endless columns of OMON, Belarus's riot police forces, surrounded the demonstrators. Mostly burly, imposing men, not all in standard uniform; some wore dark civilian clothes while most covered their faces with balaclavas—black ski masks leaving only their hostile eyes exposed. Before clashing, their hands trembled with strange involuntary twitches shaking their limbs.

On August 9, presidential elections were held. Thousands of citizens folded their ballots like accordions or wore white bracelets on their wrists. They looked at each other, knowing they were not alone. The overwhelming majority had united to vote for Svetlana Tikhanovskaya, who stepped up for the presidency after other opposition candidates—including her husband—were imprisoned.

On February 27, 2022, elections were held once more, this time to approve a new Constitution granting Lukashenko potential presidential term extensions, establishing the All-Belarusian People's Assembly, and granting judicial immunity to «any former president» while stripping neutrality and non-nuclear status clauses.

Official tallies claimed Lukashenko swept 80% of the vote. The elections were blatantly rigged, and everyone knew it. «It was an utter insult that they would lie to us so brazenly». Juilu Kevrot compares contemporary policy to Soviet rule, emphasizing that outside the capital, many families can afford only bare subsistence on bread and potatoes. Unofficial online exit polls had shown 97% voting against the regime.

Civil society exploded. Demonstrators flooded streets nationwide, workers downed tools at state factories, and pensioners marched alongside students. Lukashenko met peaceful dissent with ruthless state terror. Special forces deployed tear gas, stun grenades, and live ammunition. Thousands were dragged into detention centers, where beatings and torture were documented by international human rights bodies.

Yulia Matuzova, a former journalist at Euroradio, emphasizes: «A prison in Belarus is a place where you lose every single human right». Inmates endure systematic torture and humiliation, denied access to lawyers, family calls, or mail. Guards dump buckets of water mixed with chlorine into cells. Detainees are deprived of food and water, sleeping on cold concrete floors. Forty prisoners share just two liters of drinking water per day. Hygiene supplies and medical care are nonexistent, even for pregnant women.

Protests persisted through sheer courage. Women formed human chains, holding white flowers and singing folk anthems against the black-clad riot police. When the regime shut down internet connectivity, underground networks circulated crucial updates and protest coordinates.

No authoritarian maneuver could prevent independent journalists from documenting the truth. Many reporters were targeted; Yulia Matuzova was detained twice. One Saturday in September, while covering a peaceful women's march of protesters dressed in white carrying flowers, two plainclothes men «looking like bandits» forced her into a minibus, holding her for thirty minutes before releasing her without explanation after inspecting her press credentials.

Western sanctions and international condemnations followed, yet Lukashenko entrenched his regime through Russian financial and military patronage. The country was steadily subordinated into a staging ground for Russian strategic interests, ultimately facilitating logistics and missile strikes during the invasion of Ukraine.

Severe prison sentences hang over activists and journalists. Yulia Matuzova is certain she would currently be in a penal colony had she not fled into exile. Authorities opened a criminal prosecution against her, alleging she administered the Telegram channel Punishers of Belarus. Last December, police raided her parents' home, seizing electronics and personal files. She maintains her innocence: «I never could have imagined being branded a criminal in my own country».

Fabricating charges remains trivial for Belarusian authorities. Yiv Tesakovskiy notes that official pretexts are hardly necessary: his father was sentenced to two months' imprisonment simply for participating in a march. Having marched alongside him, Yiv fears immediate arrest if he visits, suspecting border authorities maintain files on his activities abroad: «I fear for my life».

Juilu Kevrot seeks resettlement abroad to continue his education and work, resolute in his conviction: «I never want to return». Many others lack that freedom—imprisoned, intimidated, or killed. Belarus remains under dictatorial rule, but its people continue to believe in freedom and await their reckoning.
